Almost every company that had a good day was selling into a shortage somebody else created, and almost every company that had a bad one was paying for a shortage it could not pass on. That single split explains India's largest results day of the year, a record diesel margin, and the electricity India generated and then threw away.

◆ 01. Everything that went up today was selling into a shortage somebody else created

One split runs through the entire day. Sell into a shortage and the quarter was extraordinary. Buy from one and it was not.

The clearest pattern on the largest Indian results day of the season was not growth, and it was not value. It was position. Companies that make something the world has run short of filed numbers that look almost unreal. Companies that buy something the world has run short of filed the opposite, on the same day, in the same market, often in the same sector. Once you sort the day that way, almost nothing else needs explaining.

A shortage is not the same thing as strong demand, and the difference is who gets to set the price. When demand is strong, a supplier grows alongside its customer and keeps roughly the margin it had. When there is a real shortage, the supplier decides what the customer pays, because the customer has nowhere else to go. That is why profit moved so much further than sales did across today's filings, and why so many margin lines jumped by five or ten points in a single quarter.

One company shows both sides of this at once. Sansera Engineering said its aerospace and defence order book had grown from about ₹4,400 Cr to ₹5,700 Cr in a day, on a single order from one of the world's large semiconductor equipment makers, because very few firms anywhere can machine those parts to that tolerance. The same management then explained that the thing holding the company back is how long it takes for a new precision machine to be delivered. They are short of the exact thing they sell (Section 3).

The same shape appears far outside India, in places that have nothing to do with each other. The margin on turning crude oil into diesel hit a record, while the oil itself is not scarce. Indian power stations threw away clean electricity they had already generated, because the wires to move it do not exist. In both cases the physical thing is available and the step in the middle is not, and that middle step is where all the money went.

What this means for reading the rest of today is simple. A very good quarter is not proof of a very good business, because a shortage ends when somebody builds more of the missing thing. The useful question on every number below is whether the company is short of something because it built an advantage, or because the world has not caught up yet. The answers are different, and they have different lifespans.

◆ 02. India's biggest results day of the year split cleanly into who could pass on a cost and who could not NEW

More than 150 companies filed at once. Sorting them by margin rather than by sales gives a much cleaner answer than sorting them by sector.

The volume of Indian results today was unusual even for a results season. More than 150 companies filed in one window, and the breadth of the good ones was wide enough that people who watch this every quarter said they could not remember a day like it. That breadth is the story, because when this many companies beat at once it is usually something happening to costs rather than something happening to demand.

The winners were mostly companies that sell into a supply gap and were able to keep the whole price increase. Solar Industries India lifted profit to ₹666 Cr from ₹353 Cr with its margin widening on the way, on defence explosives that very few firms are cleared to make. Kennametal India nearly tripled profit on cutting tools. Galaxy Surfactants roughly doubled its operating profit on a sales increase of about a third. The pattern repeats in Ipca Laboratories, Genus Power Infrastructures, Modison and Foseco India.

The losers were companies standing in the middle of a chain, buying at the new price and selling at the old one. Indraprastha Gas is the cleanest case, with revenue up but operating profit down to ₹294 Cr from ₹511 Cr, because it buys gas at the market price and sells it to households at a price it cannot move quickly. Avanti Feeds, Bajaj Hindusthan Sugar, Kaveri Seed Company and Everest Kanto Cylinder all show the same squeeze in different industries.

The single largest company to file today split in half, and the two halves went opposite ways. Tata Motors commercial vehicles more than doubled profit to about ₹2,560 Cr on higher dispatches and a domestic share near 37%, because trucks are being bought by people who need to move goods now. The passenger vehicle arm saw profit after minority interest, which is the profit left once outside shareholders in subsidiaries take their share, fall to about ₹775 Cr from ₹3,924 Cr. Same group, same country, same day.

A warning worth carrying into every number below. Several companies published two versions of themselves today. SKF India filed a revenue fall of 54% and a revenue rise of 27% on the identical revenue figure of ₹588 Cr, because one is the consolidated business and one is the standalone parent. Honasa Consumer put three different growth rates on one announcement. Quoting whichever number is loudest is how a good quarter and a bad quarter get confused.

Read against the rest of the day, this results table is the domestic version of a global trade. The Indian companies that did well are, almost without exception, selling shovels into somebody else's construction project, whether that project is a defence order, a power grid or a factory. The ones that did badly are the ones who had to buy from those same shovel sellers. That is the same split that shows up in oil, in electricity and in computing power over the next few sections.

◆ 03. The most valuable machine in India right now is the one that makes other machines DEVELOPING

Precision engineering had the strongest day of any Indian sector, and the limit on all of it is how long a new machine tool takes to arrive.

A cluster of small Indian engineering firms reported numbers today that do not look like an ordinary quarter. These are companies that machine metal to tolerances measured in microns, for aircraft engines, defence programmes and semiconductor equipment. Nobody buys from them because they are cheap. They get bought from because the list of firms anywhere that can hold that tolerance is very short, and it is not getting longer quickly.

The clearest number came from Sansera Engineering, which crossed ₹1,000 Cr of quarterly revenue for the first time. Revenue rose about a third and operating profit rose about half, which is the margin signature of a company that is being asked rather than pitching. Its aerospace, defence and semiconductor revenue was roughly three times what it was a year ago, and now makes up about a fifth of the company.

The order book is where the caution belongs. The investor presentation put the aerospace and defence backlog at about ₹4,437 Cr, and on the call the same day management said it had gone to ₹5,700 Cr on one large semiconductor equipment order that runs out to 2031. Both figures are work promised, not work done. A backlog stretching five years is a good problem and a slow one, and it will show up as revenue long after this quarter is forgotten.

The constraint is the same for everybody in the group, and management said it plainly. The thing that sets the timeline in ultra high precision machining is how long it takes the mother machine, the machine tool that makes the parts, to be delivered. Money does not fix that, and neither does an order book. It is the reason a shortage in this business lasts years rather than quarters, and the reason a competitor cannot simply decide to enter.

The smaller names in the same trade moved even harder. OBSC Perfection grew revenue about 85% with profit up rather more, on an order book above ₹1,200 Cr, and its forging line is running at roughly 30% of capacity, so most of that book has somewhere to go. Kernex Microsystems printed revenue of about ₹504 Cr against ₹56 Cr a year ago on train collision avoidance work, though its own order book shrank about 12% in the quarter, which is what happens when you execute faster than you win.

The read across to the rest of the digest is uncomfortable for the bigger promises. India spent today announcing aircraft programmes, magnet plants and data centres that all depend on exactly this kind of machining capacity existing by the time they need it (Section 9). The companies that own the machines had a very good day. The programmes that will need them are still tenders.

◆ 04. India is not short of electricity, it is short of wire DEVELOPING

Peak demand crossed 270 GW and supply held. The power that was wasted was wasted because it could not be moved.

India's electricity system passed a real test and failed a quieter one on the same day. Peak demand crossed 270 GW for the first time, about 70% above where it was a decade ago, and the grid largely held. At the same time the country threw away 300 GWh of clean electricity in the first quarter of this year because there was no way to move it from where it was made to where it was wanted.

That second number is the one that matters, because it is a different kind of problem. Building more generation is now the easy part in India, and it is happening fast. Building the lines to carry it is slow, contested and unglamorous, and until those lines exist every new solar farm partly competes with the ones already built for the same limited path to a customer.

The fix that is actually being funded is the cheap one. Power Grid Corporation of India approved about ₹857 Cr to restring two existing 400 kV lines in Tamil Nadu with better wire, which roughly doubles what those lines can carry without building a single new tower. It takes around two years instead of the five to fifteen a new line needs, and there is no land to acquire. It is also the least visible kind of infrastructure spending there is.

The equipment makers are describing the same shortage from the other end. One transmission tower maker expects bids worth roughly ₹90,000 Cr to ₹1 lakh crore in the Indian transmission market this financial year. A power equipment maker described a severe shortage of gas turbines, gas engines, transformers and generators. Those are order books forming, not revenue, and they are the reason Genus Power Infrastructures and GE Power India both printed strong quarters today.

The most useful thing anybody said about power storage today was a warning about its economics. Amara Raja Energy and Mobility is spending roughly ₹1,300 Cr of this year's capital on new energy, largely a battery storage plant, and its own management guided operating margins on that business of only around 5% to 7%, perhaps 7% to 8% at best, because everybody in India will be competing with imports rather than with each other. A manufacturer talking down its own incentive scheme is worth more than a forecast.

This is where India's power story and the global compute story meet. The same physical bottleneck, moving electricity to a specific place at a specific time, is what is now rationing artificial intelligence in the United States, where roughly 72% of the power requested for data centres is not expected to ever be delivered. India is discovering the constraint at 270 GW of ordinary demand. America is discovering it with a queue of buyers who can pay almost anything.

◆ 05. The oil shock is showing up in diesel, not in crude DEVELOPING

The margin on turning oil into diesel hit a record while the oil itself is not scarce. That gap is a refining problem wearing an oil crisis costume.

The most important energy price today was not the price of oil. It was the gap between what a barrel of crude costs and what the diesel made from it sells for, which reached a record near $100 a barrel. That gap is pure refining margin, and it widens when the world has plenty of crude and not enough working capacity to turn it into fuel.

The reason this matters is that the two problems have completely different fixes. If crude were scarce, producers could pump more and the price would settle. If refining is the bottleneck, pumping more does nothing at all, and the cost lands on everybody who moves goods, farms, or runs a generator. Diesel is the fuel of physical activity, so a record diesel margin is a tax on every business that ships something.

The demand data quietly contradicts the crisis framing. The main global energy agency revised last year's oil consumption up to about 104.8 million barrels a day, and revised supply to roughly 106.3 million, meaning the world was oversupplied with crude even while the headlines were about a strait being closed. Its new forecast for next year is now higher than the peak level it predicted a year ago for the end of this decade.

What actually happened around the Strait of Hormuz remains genuinely unclear, and that is worth saying plainly. American officials have described flows returning to roughly normal, tanker tracking data does not support that, and Iranian state media says no ship transits without approval. Three accounts, one waterway, and no way to reconcile them from outside. The refining margin is the observable fact, and it is behaving as though the disruption is real and ongoing.

Several separate physical accidents landed in the same window and none of them were market events. A fatal explosion hit a fuel trading site in Rotterdam, a blast hit an Italian munitions plant central to Europe's ammunition supply, and a strike on a Russian Black Sea port shut grain terminals and pushed wheat up. Any one of these is a news item. Together they describe a system with no slack in it.

The link back to the Indian results is direct. Every company in Section 2 that could not pass on a cost is, one or two steps back, paying for this diesel margin, whether through freight, packaging or power. The Indian refiners and explosives makers sit on the other side of it. A record refining spread is not an oil story, it is a redistribution from everybody who moves things to the small number of people who process them.

◆ 06. America's largest spending line is now the interest on what it already borrowed NEW

Interest has passed both defence and Medicare. The thirty year bond was sold at the highest yield since 2001.

The United States government now spends more on interest than on its military or on healthcare for the elderly. Interest cost $827 bn in the first nine months of this fiscal year, which annualises to roughly $1.38 trillion, or about 4.2% of the whole economy. That share is the highest since 1997, and five years ago it was little more than half of it.

The reason this compounds rather than just growing is that old cheap debt keeps maturing into new expensive debt. The Treasury rolls over around $6 trillion every three months, so each quarter a large slice of borrowing from the low rate years gets replaced at today's rates. Nothing has to go wrong for the number to keep climbing. It climbs simply because time passes.

The bond market made that point in the clearest way available today. Thirty year debt was sold at a yield of 5.216%, the highest at auction since 2001, and the ten year sold at the highest since 2007. These are not panic levels, and there was no failed auction. They are the price the government now has to pay to find buyers, and that price is being set higher each time it asks.

July's monthly numbers show how much of the spending is now borrowed rather than earned. The deficit for the single month was about $432 bn, the largest July on record, and roughly 56 cents of every dollar the government spent was borrowed. Total debt outstanding sits just under $40 trillion, a threshold it will cross within days.

Who is buying is changing in a way that matters more than the level. Foreign central banks and sovereign funds have stepped back, and their place is increasingly taken by hedge funds borrowing heavily against short term money markets to hold Treasuries. That works while funding is cheap and calm. It is a very different buyer from a central bank that holds a bond because it wants the dollars, and it behaves very differently when something goes wrong.

This sits underneath every equity number in the next section. A stock market at a record while the risk free rate is being auctioned at a twenty five year high means investors are being paid less to take risk than at almost any point in living memory. That is not an argument that anything is about to break. It is the reason the record itself is worth less than it looks.

◆ 07. A record American stock market was bought by almost everyone except the people paid to manage money NEW

The index closed at an all time high in the same week institutions sold the largest amount of technology futures ever recorded.

The main American index closed above 7,800 for the first time, and the money behind that move came from an unusual place. In the week to early August, institutions sold a record $21.6 bn of Nasdaq futures, with short positions making up nearly three quarters of it. Combined institutional positioning turned negative for the first time since May last year, having been strongly positive as recently as October.

Somebody else absorbed all of that, and it is fairly clear who. Company buybacks are running above a trillion dollars, individual investors have returned, and rule based funds that buy automatically when volatility falls have been rebuilding. None of those three buyers is making a judgement about value. They are all buying because a formula, a board authorisation or a habit says to.

The most striking detail of the day is how much of the market is now leveraged bets rather than ownership. Leveraged exchange traded funds are about 1% of the global fund industry by assets and a record 16% of daily trading value. Their average daily volume has tripled since January. Artificial intelligence names are a record 58% of long leveraged exposure, up from 26% four years ago, and memory chip makers alone are almost half of the single stock leveraged trade.

The inflation print that triggered the rally deserves a second look. Producer prices were flat month on month, the first flat reading in over a year, which markets read as the all clear. The single most inflationary component within it was portfolio management fees, which rise when the stock market rises. The measure went cold partly because assets went up, which is a circular kind of comfort.

One stock carried an implausible share of the move. SpaceX rose about 35% in five sessions after its first share lockup expired, adding roughly half a trillion dollars of market value, on the reasoning that the shares released did not crush the price. The company is now being valued largely as a computing business, on a plan to add six to ten gigawatts of data centres in a single year, which is a promise about electricity as much as about rockets (Section 4).

The honest summary is that the record is real and the conviction behind it is not. Prices are set by whoever trades last, not by whoever believes most, and today the last trades came from automatic buyers while the discretionary money was going the other way. That is a market that can keep rising and also has very little underneath it if the automatic bid pauses.

◆ 08. The best open artificial intelligence model is now within a rounding error of the best closed one, at a fraction of the price NEW

A Chinese lab shipped a model that scores 87.9 where the leading closed model scores 88.0, and gave away the software around it.

The gap between the best model anybody can download and the best model anybody can rent effectively closed today. On a widely used test of whether a model can complete real multi step computer tasks, the newly released open model scored 87.9 against 88.0 for the leading closed frontier model. One tenth of a point is not a difference, it is a tie.

Price is where the two are still nothing like each other. Depending on how it is measured the open model runs somewhere between roughly 13 and 17 times cheaper per unit of work than its closest open rival, and further below the closed frontier again. The same lab also released the software harness that drives the model as an open licence, which means the surrounding tooling is free as well.

The quality caveat is real and is being skipped in most retellings. On a security benchmark the model found 87.5% of the known flaws it was asked to find, ahead of the closed leaders. Only about two thirds of the vulnerabilities it reported were valid, against roughly 86% for the best closed model. It is better at finding things and much worse at knowing when it is wrong, which is a very different product for anything serious.

Prices are not uniformly falling, and that undercuts the simple story. The same lab has raised its prices four separate times, while closed frontier models have been getting cheaper. If that continues, the comfortable assumption that open models are permanently the cheap option, and that the enormous funding commitments behind closed labs are safe because prices hold, is the thing that gets tested.

The money around all of this kept moving at a scale detached from revenue. A data platform company closed a $5 bn round at a $190 bn valuation, one leading American lab is in talks to buy a video model startup for around $6 bn, and its investors expect a listing later this year that would be the largest ever. None of those numbers are earnings. They are bets on where the earnings go.

The connection to the physical sections above is the whole point. If frontier level intelligence costs a fraction of what it did, the scarce input stops being the model and becomes the electricity and the buildings to run it, which is exactly the constraint India is hitting at 270 GW and America is hitting in its interconnection queue. Cheap intelligence makes power expensive.

◆ 09. The most interesting Indian deals today were about who owns the lender, not who builds the factory NEW

Foreign money is buying its way into Indian credit at size, while the industrial announcements remain tenders and bids.

The largest Indian transaction of the day was a foreign bank buying half of a lender that barely existed three years ago. Bank of America agreed to put up to about ₹18,268 Cr into Jio Credit, roughly ₹6,613 Cr upfront for 26.5% and warrants taking it towards 49.9%, subject to regulatory approval. The lender's loan book has grown about 2.6 times in a year and its profit more than doubled.

It is the fourth or fifth deal of its kind in a short space of time, which makes it a pattern rather than an event. A Gulf bank has put money into one Indian bank, an American private equity firm into another, a Japanese bank into a large finance company, and a sovereign fund into a housing lender. Foreign institutions are not buying Indian factories at this scale. They are buying the balance sheets that lend to them.

A much stranger deal happened at the other end of the market. A large private building materials group is arranging to reach the stock market through Shalimar Paints, a small loss making paint company, using new shares and convertible instruments worth roughly ₹10,500 Cr against a company that reported quarterly revenue of ₹138 Cr and a loss. That is a listing achieved by absorption rather than by public offer, and it puts several well known investors onto a paint company's share register.

The index changes will move more money than either deal. A major global index provider added Adani Energy Solutions, Laurus Labs, Lenskart Solutions and the parent of a large broking platform to one of its India indices, with a separate quarterly review adding fourteen names including Astral, Ather Energy, Dalmia Bharat and Urban Company and removing nineteen others. Passive funds have to buy and sell those names regardless of what they think of them.

The industrial announcements, by contrast, were almost all still at the paper stage. India invited bids for sixty military transport aircraft worth around ₹1 lakh crore, opened a rare earth magnet scheme that drew fifteen bids, and put ₹10,000 Cr behind domestic shipping container manufacturing. Selection on the aircraft alone is expected to take more than two years. The one industrial item that is real is an order for a large computing cluster in Chennai, and even its value is reported in a range rather than confirmed.

Set against Section 3, the split is clear and slightly uncomfortable. The money that arrived today went to lending and to index membership, both of which can be deployed immediately. The money that was announced for machines, aircraft and magnets goes to things that need the precision manufacturing capacity that is already fully booked. The financial system is moving considerably faster than the physical one.

THE EDGE

The thing nobody said out loud today is that Sansera is simultaneously the best example of the shortage trade and the clearest evidence of its ceiling. Its order book jumped by about ₹1,300 Cr in a day because almost nobody can do the work, and its own management said the binding limit is how long a new precision machine takes to arrive. That means the order book can grow faster than the company can ever convert it, which looks like enormous growth in a backlog slide and like a slowly rising revenue line in the accounts.

Applied more widely, this is the reason the shortage trade is safer than it looks and slower than it is priced. The barrier that lets these companies charge more is the same barrier that stops them growing quickly, and it is made of physical equipment with multi year lead times. A competitor cannot arrive next quarter, and neither can the revenue.

SO WHAT FOR A NORMAL READER

If you hold Indian small and mid cap industrial names, the number worth checking this quarter is not sales growth, it is whether the operating margin widened at the same time. A company growing sales while its margin shrinks is passing money to somebody upstream, and today produced a lot of both kinds.

If you are watching the artificial intelligence story, the thing that changed today is that the model stopped being the expensive part. Attention is worth moving to electricity, transmission and land, because that is where the constraint now sits, in India and in the United States alike.

If you are simply trying to read results season, treat any company that published two different versions of the same quarter as needing a second look rather than a quick conclusion. Several did today, and in more than one case the two versions pointed in opposite directions.
